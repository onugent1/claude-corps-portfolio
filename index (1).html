Patient Care

Where the instinct started

I volunteered at Baylor Scott & White Hospital for two years before I ever started my clinical career. I delivered flowers to a woman recovering from surgery and watched her whole day change over something that small. On another day, I led a family through the back way connecting the main BSW hospital and the BSW heart hospital during a rainstorm so they wouldn't have to walk it in the rain, and it made their day. Neither moment was a big deal on paper. Both of them stuck with me, and they're a big part of why I kept choosing healthcare after that.

I spent four years as a Medical Scribe, Patient Care Assistant, and ER Technician at Northwest Arkansas Pediatrics and Arkansas Children's Northwest Hospital. Working through the COVID-19 pandemic showed me exactly where care breaks down operationally: a missed handoff, information that doesn't reach the right person, an update that comes too late.

Even before that, I'd spent years in high school organizing donations for Love Truck Ministry: sorting goods into the right containers, collecting from people, making sure everything was accounted for. It wasn't glamorous work, but showing up consistently for people was never new to me.

Community

Dream BIG: what a year of mentoring actually looked like

During undergrad at the University of Arkansas, I mentored a girl at a Springdale middle school through Dream BIG, a program pairing university women with mentees for a year of "Brave Girl" sessions built around identity and community. We met in person once a month, texted every week, threw holiday parties, and I'd stop by her school at lunch just to talk and meet her friends.

Some months we ran activities, like a word-of-affirmations project aimed at building self-confidence. One session was about college preparedness. My mentee told me she didn't understand why going to college even mattered. Instead of arguing with her, I told her my own story: what I actually did to apply, what it was like to be a college student, how fun it was but also how empowering it felt to gain knowledge of the world on my own terms. Studying Kinesiology in college is where I built the knowledge and skills I needed to pursue a career in healthcare. I wanted her to hear that college wasn't just a requirement, it was something that could genuinely open up her future, and I wanted to motivate her to do her best in school so she could get there.

Not every session had a clear topic to build a lesson around. In one of our conversations, she opened up about something harder to fix than a school assignment.

"My mentee told me she didn't have any friends. I didn't want to tell her how to navigate her friendships. But she needed to learn the difference between friends who aren't there for you and true friendships, the kind where people show up for each other. I didn't always know the right thing to say, but I told her that being 'popular' doesn't matter. Be true to yourself, and true friendships will follow."

Being an older sister is probably where a lot of my instincts for that came from. Mentoring her is also where I first felt, clearly, how much I care about making a real difference in one person's life.

Community

Lifestyles Inc.: adapting when the room went quiet

Through a school internship program, I volunteered twice a week at Lifestyles Inc. in Fayetteville, an organization that supports adults with disabilities. One day a week I assisted an art class where participants used the sessions to express themselves, and even took it on themselves to clean up after, small proof of the independence the program was built to nurture. The other day, I helped with college-style classes at the University of Arkansas teaching basic living skills: managing money, understanding emotions, and taking on responsibility.

When COVID-19 hit, in-person classes weren't possible anymore, so I ran a class over Zoom instead. It didn't work at first. Students were distracted and had a hard time paying attention, discouraged and silent, cameras on but nobody asking a question after the lesson. I started building the lessons around what they actually cared about, referencing their favorite superheroes and movies, and calling people out by name instead of waiting for hands to raise. I also built science experiments students could do at home and taught a class on how color affects our emotions and the decisions we make, since that was easier to make personal on a screen than in a lecture.

It taught me that engagement isn't something a lesson plan guarantees. If a room goes quiet, that's information, not a dead end, and the fix is usually to make the material about the person in front of you before you try to make it about the content.

What made the whole experience stick with me, though, was the friendships. I got to know the students well enough that Night to Shine, the prom night held for people with disabilities, wasn't just an event I volunteered for, it was a night out with people I actually cared about. Watching them get dressed up and genuinely enjoy themselves is something I still think about. I'm grateful I got to know them.

Explaining The Technical To The Non-Technical

A pattern that goes back further than this year

Long before Claude, I was already the person translating something technical or unfamiliar for someone who needed it in plain terms:

  • Training my own replacement as a Medical Scribe, teaching them clinical documentation habits and how to use the EHR system so the provider could work efficiently and charting corrections stayed low at the end of the day.
  • Training newly hired Patient Care Assistants on how the clinic workflow worked, how to run laboratory tests, and how to juggle multiple tasks at once during a high-volume patient day.
  • Teaching kids to swim as a high school swim instructor, where the audience was young, sometimes scared, and had zero shared vocabulary to start from.

It's the same skill each time: take something I understand and find the version of it that actually lands for the person in front of me.
